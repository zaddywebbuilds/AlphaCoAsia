Singapore insurers now face a statutory regime in which proliferation financing sits alongside money laundering and terrorism financing in the Insurance Act, and the penalties for getting it wrong have been raised. Trade press on 20 November 2026 reported penalties of up to S$250,000 for contraventions, with additional daily penalties for continuing breaches. Confirm the exact figures against the gazetted text before you quote them to your board, because the amounts and the mechanics are what a regulator will test. This article explains what has changed, what the proliferation financing Insurance Act penalties Singapore insurers should plan for look like in practice, and which controls to fix before year-end.

## What the amendment does

The Financial Services and Markets (Amendment) Act 2026 extends the supervisory mandate of the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) from AML/CFT to AML/CFT/CPF, where CPF denotes countering proliferation financing. Reporting indicates the Act was passed by Parliament in October 2026 and gazetted in November 2026. The Act also makes conforming amendments to a number of sector statutes, including the Insurance Act 1966. In the Bill text, one visible change in the Insurance Act replaces a reference to AML/CFT with AML/CFT/CPF in the provision dealing with MAS directions and requirements.

The practical consequence is that the legal basis for MAS to issue binding requirements, inspect and enforce on proliferation financing is now explicit in the insurance statute, not inferred from terrorism financing provisions or from the general power to issue notices. For a compliance officer, that means a gap on proliferation financing can no longer be treated as a guideline-level weakness. It is a statutory matter.

Check the commencement position first. An Act that is passed and gazetted does not necessarily apply in full from the date of gazette. Sections are often brought into force by ministerial notification on dates that differ by provision. Ask your legal counsel to confirm which provisions are in force today and which are scheduled.

## How this builds on the 2025 MAS revisions

MAS had already moved proliferation financing into its expectations for insurers. The revisions that took effect on 1 July 2025 expressly brought proliferation financing into the enterprise-wide risk assessment and control framework, and sharpened expectations on suspicious transaction reporting. Our earlier briefing on the [2025 AML/CFT updates for Singapore insurers](/insights/mas-aml-cft-2025-revisions-insurers-proliferation-financing) sets out that gap assessment in detail.

The new Act changes the enforcement context, not the underlying control expectations. Before the Act, a weak proliferation financing assessment was a supervisory finding under notices and guidelines. After the Act, the same weakness sits against an express statutory objective, and the penalty regime reported in the press gives MAS a clearer route to financial consequences.

Life insurers are bound by MAS Notice 314, which is legally binding. General insurers and reinsurers have historically worked from MAS guidelines. Do not assume the lower-risk reputation of general business removes the proliferation financing question. Trade finance, marine cargo, political risk, trade credit and certain specialty lines carry exposure to sanctioned jurisdictions, dual-use goods and shipping networks, and these are exactly the lines where proliferation financing typologies appear.

## Reading the penalty regime

Treat the S$250,000 figure and the daily penalty feature as the planning case until you have read the provision. Three questions matter when you do.

First, to whom does the penalty apply: the licensed insurer, its officers, or both? Officer liability changes how a board and senior management should document their oversight.

Second, which contraventions attract the penalty: failure to comply with a MAS direction, failure to implement required controls, or failure to provide information? The answer determines which controls evidence you must be able to produce on request.

Third, how does a daily penalty accrue? If continuing breaches attract a further amount for each day, a known gap that remains open after discovery is materially more expensive than the same gap closed promptly. This is the strongest argument for an immediate remediation tracker with named owners and dates.

Financial penalties are not the only consequence. MAS can also take other supervisory action, and reputational damage from a public enforcement outcome affects relationships with reinsurers, banks and distribution partners.

## Controls to review before year-end

Boards should not wait for the final shape of subsidiary requirements. The following steps are defensible under any reading of the Act.

Update the enterprise-wide risk assessment. It should assess proliferation financing as a distinct risk, separate from money laundering and terrorism financing, with its own inherent risk factors by product, customer, geography and distribution channel. Document the rationale where you conclude exposure is low.

Review sanctions and targeted financial sanctions screening. Proliferation financing controls rest heavily on screening customers, beneficiaries, payees and, where relevant, vessels and counterparties against the lists MAS requires insurers to use. Confirm list update frequency, the handling of name-match alerts, and the escalation route for a potential true match, including the obligation to freeze and report.

Map policy features to typologies. Single-premium products, early surrender requests, third-party premium payers, assignments and unusual claims payments to unrelated parties are indicators your transaction monitoring rules should address. For general lines, review marine, trade credit and political risk underwriting for sanctions and dual-use exposure.

Test suspicious transaction reporting. Confirm your reporting path handles proliferation financing suspicion as a trigger, and that staff understand the tipping-off restrictions. Our guide to [suspicious transaction reporting for life insurers](/insights/suspicious-transaction-reporting-life-insurers-singapore-2025-revisions) covers the process in more detail.

Refresh training. Front line, claims, underwriting and compliance staff need examples relevant to insurance, not generic banking material.

## Board and senior management responsibilities

Boards are expected to set the tone and approve the risk appetite for financial crime, and senior management must implement and evidence it. Under a regime with express penalties, the quality of the paper trail becomes decisive. Minutes should show that the board received a proliferation financing assessment, challenged it, and approved a remediation plan with dates. The compliance function should report open gaps and closure progress at each meeting until the position is clean.

Group insurers should also test whether the group standard covers proliferation financing to the level required in Singapore. A group policy calibrated to another jurisdiction may omit a Singapore requirement or assign responsibilities in a way that does not match the local structure. A short applicability matrix signed off by the Singapore compliance officer is a practical safeguard.

Finally, plan for independent assurance. Internal audit or an external reviewer should test whether the controls operate as designed, not just whether the policy exists. Findings from that test are valuable evidence if MAS asks how you identified and fixed gaps.

## Next steps

A sensible sequence is: confirm commencement dates with counsel, read the penalty provisions in the gazetted Act, refresh the enterprise-wide risk assessment, run a screening and monitoring effectiveness test, and present a remediation plan to the board before the year-end cycle.
