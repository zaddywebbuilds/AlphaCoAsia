The MAS compliance toolkit for insurers is most useful when you stop treating it as a reference document and convert it into a working calendar. Each toolkit lists the approvals, notifications and submissions that a licensed entity owes MAS, but it does not tell you who owns each item, when the internal preparation must start or how the obligations cluster across the year. Compliance teams that build that layer themselves avoid the late filings and missed notifications that attract supervisory attention.

This walkthrough explains what the toolkits contain, how to read them, and how to build an annual obligations register for a direct insurer, reinsurer or captive. It is written for compliance officers, company secretaries and finance leads at Singapore-licensed insurers who want a defensible process rather than a one-off read-through.

## What the MAS Toolkits Are and Who They Cover

MAS publishes separate compliance toolkits for different categories of regulated insurance entity. The categories include direct insurers, captive insurers and special purpose reinsurance vehicles. MAS has revised these toolkits periodically, and the 2026 revisions mean that any internal compliance register built on an earlier version should be re-checked against the current document.

Each toolkit consolidates, in one place, the requirements that flow from the Insurance Act 1966 and its subsidiary legislation and MAS notices and guidelines. For an entity in scope this typically means the Insurance (Valuation and Capital) Regulations, MAS Notice 133 on valuation and capital, MAS Notice 126 on enterprise risk management, the corporate governance and fit and proper requirements, and the AML/CFT notices that apply to the entity's business line. The exact list differs by category, which is why you should always work from the toolkit that matches your licence type. Our comparison of [Singapore insurer licence types](/insights/singapore-insurer-licence-types-direct-reinsurer-captive) explains how those categories differ.

A toolkit is a summary aid, not a substitute for the underlying law. Where the toolkit and a notice appear to differ, the notice governs. Your register should therefore cite the source instrument for every line, not just the toolkit page.

## Reading the Toolkit as Three Separate Lists

The most practical step is to split the toolkit into three lists, because each type of obligation is managed differently.

The first list is approvals. These are matters where you must obtain MAS approval before acting. Typical examples include the appointment of directors, the chief executive and other key appointment holders, certain changes in controlling shareholders, and the appointment of an Appointed Actuary where one is required. Approvals are driven by internal events, so the control is a pre-clearance gate: no appointment letter, board resolution or share transfer is finalised until compliance confirms whether MAS approval or prior notice is needed.

The second list is notifications. These are events that must be reported to MAS within a set period after they occur or are discovered. Examples include significant breaches, material changes to the business plan or reinsurance arrangements, and certain changes to key personnel. Notifications are triggered by facts, often discovered by people outside compliance, so the control is an escalation route that makes sure the facts reach you in time.

The third list is periodic submissions: regulatory returns, the ORSA report, audited financial statements, actuarial reports and similar items. These follow a fixed timetable, so the control is a calendar with internal deadlines set well ahead of the MAS deadline.

Keeping the three lists apart prevents the common failure where a notification obligation is filed under the annual calendar and only noticed when a quarterly review happens to catch it.

## Building the Annual Obligations Calendar

Once you have the three lists, build the calendar in four steps.

First, map each periodic submission to its regulatory deadline and then work backwards to set three internal dates: first draft, sign-off by the accountable executive, and board or committee approval where required. For an ORSA report submitted under Notice 126, board approval has to fall before submission, so the board calendar is a dependency. The recent MAS circular on [ORSA report submission lapses](/insights/mas-circular-id-01-26-orsa-submission-lapses) is a reminder that late or incomplete submissions are a recognised supervisory concern.

Second, assign a named owner and a named deputy to every line. A function title is not an owner. Where the Appointed Actuary, the chief risk officer or the finance head is the real preparer, record that, and record who in compliance verifies.

Third, add the dependencies. Annual audited accounts depend on the audit timetable; the actuarial valuation depends on data cut-off; the capital position reported under Notice 133 depends on both. If a dependency slips, the register should show immediately which MAS deadline is now at risk.

Fourth, add event-driven items as a standing checklist alongside the calendar, with the trigger events written in plain language that non-compliance staff recognise, such as a director resignation, a change in a reinsurance panel, a data incident or a breach of a regulatory limit.

## Special Considerations for Captive Insurers

A captive insurer faces a lighter set of obligations than a direct insurer, but the lighter load creates its own risk. Captives are often run by a small team or by a third-party manager, and compliance can become an afterthought. The captive insurer compliance toolkit is a good starting point for a manager who needs to confirm exactly what the captive owes MAS and what the parent owes on the captive's behalf.

Three points deserve attention. First, confirm which obligations the captive manager performs under the management agreement and which stay with the captive board. Delegation of the task does not delegate accountability. Second, check how the captive's risk management and solvency documentation is scaled to its business, since MAS applies requirements proportionately but still expects a coherent framework. Third, confirm that restrictions on the captive's business, such as limits on writing third-party risks, are embedded in underwriting authorities so that an accidental breach cannot arise through a routine renewal.

The same discipline applies to special purpose reinsurance vehicles, where the key risks are usually documentation, reporting to investors and maintaining the structural conditions on which the vehicle was approved.

## Notifications: Where Most Teams Fall Short

Periodic filings are visible and well resourced. Notifications are where compliance programmes tend to leak. The toolkit will list the events MAS expects to hear about, but a list does not create the behaviour.

Build three controls. A short escalation protocol, circulated to business heads, that states which events must reach compliance within one working day. A log of potential reportable events, including those assessed as not reportable and the reasoning, so that the judgement can be defended later. And a template notification with the information MAS usually needs: what happened, when it was discovered, the impact, the cause if known, and the remediation steps and timeline.

Where an event falls under the technology risk or incident reporting requirements, align your protocol with the separate incident reporting timelines rather than treating it as a general compliance matter. ## Keeping the Register Current in 2026

A register is only as good as its maintenance. Set a recurring review, at least semi-annually, to compare the register against the current version of each toolkit and the underlying notices. Record the version date of the toolkit used, the reviewer and the changes made. After any MAS revision, circular or consultation outcome, update the affected lines the same month.

Link the register to your governance structure. A summary should go to the board or its risk and audit committee at least annually, showing items completed on time, items completed late with reasons, notifications made and open remediation. This gives the board evidence of oversight and gives MAS, on inspection, a clear audit trail from obligation to evidence of performance.

Finally, test the register. Pick three obligations at random each quarter and trace each from the toolkit line to the owner, the working papers and the proof of submission. Gaps found in testing are cheaper to fix than gaps found by the regulator.
