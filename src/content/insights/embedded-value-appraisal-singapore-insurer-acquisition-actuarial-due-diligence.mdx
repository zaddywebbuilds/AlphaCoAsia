An embedded value appraisal for a Singapore insurance acquisition values the target as the sum of its adjusted net asset value, the present value of profits from in-force business, and the value of new business the buyer expects to write. The number only holds if the capital held against the in-force book is measured on the MAS RBC2 basis and the profit emergence is reconciled to the IFRS 17 contractual service margin (CSM). Actuarial due diligence exists to test whether those three views agree, and to price the gaps when they do not.

## What an embedded value appraisal actually measures

Embedded value (EV) is the value of the shareholders' interest in the existing business, excluding the value of future new business. It has three building blocks:

- Adjusted net asset value (ANAV): free surplus plus required capital, adjusted to market value of assets and net of any liabilities not captured in the reserves.
- Value of in-force business (VIF): the present value of projected after-tax distributable profits from policies already written, less the cost of holding required capital and the time value of financial options and guarantees.
- Appraisal value: EV plus goodwill, usually the present value of profits from future new business over a defined horizon.

Buyers of Singapore life insurers typically anchor price negotiation on appraisal value, often expressed as a multiple of EV or of value of new business. The seller's own EV may be published on a market-consistent basis in line with the CFO Forum principles, or on a traditional basis with a risk discount rate. Due diligence must establish which, because the two can differ materially for books with long-dated guarantees.

## Why RBC2 changes the capital leg of the valuation

Under the risk-based capital framework in the Insurance Act and MAS Notice 133 on the valuation and capital framework, an insurer's available capital is tested against a total risk requirement calibrated at a 99.5% one-year value at risk. The capital the buyer must hold to back the in-force book is therefore a function of the RBC2 risk charges, not of the target's legacy solvency position.

Three points matter in practice:

- Required capital in the VIF projection should follow the insurer's own capital policy and the MAS supervisory intervention thresholds, not simply the regulatory minimum. A buyer who values the book at the minimum will overstate distributable profit.
- RBC2 admissibility limits on Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital, and on additional Tier 1 instruments, affect how much of the target's capital stack is genuinely available to the buyer. See our analysis of [risk-based capital](/expertise/risk-based-capital) for the structure of the framework.
- Interest rate and credit spread sensitivities in the RBC2 calculation feed directly into the cost of capital charge. A target with a mismatch that looks acceptable in the accounts may carry a heavy capital requirement.

Actuarial due diligence should rebuild the projected capital requirement from the target's model, test it against the latest MAS regulatory returns, and run it forward under the buyer's intended asset strategy. If the buyer plans to change the investment mix, the capital requirement and the VIF both move.

## Reconciling EV to the IFRS 17 CSM

Singapore insurers report under SFRS(I) 17, the local adoption of IFRS 17. The CSM represents unearned profit on in-force groups of contracts and is released as services are provided. It is not the same as VIF, and treating the two as interchangeable is one of the commonest errors in acquisition models.

The differences a reviewer should isolate:

- Discounting: IFRS 17 uses a current discount rate built from observable market yields, while EV may use a risk discount rate or a risk-free rate plus a cost of capital charge.
- Risk adjustment: IFRS 17 holds an explicit risk adjustment for non-financial risk, while EV captures the same risk through the cost of required capital. The two can differ by a wide margin.
- Profit recognition: IFRS 17 releases CSM by coverage units, while EV projects distributable profit after capital movements. Timing differs even when total profit is equal.
- Acquisition accounting: after the deal, the buyer measures acquired contracts at fair value at the acquisition date, so the buyer's opening CSM will differ from the seller's closing CSM.

A reconciliation bridge from the seller's EV to the seller's IFRS 17 equity and CSM, then onward to the buyer's expected opening position, gives the board a way to explain price to its own auditors and to MAS. ## The core work of actuarial due diligence

Actuarial due diligence in insurance M&A is a structured review of the target's reserves, assumptions, models and data, with the outcome expressed as an adjustment to the headline valuation. A well-scoped engagement covers:

- Reserve adequacy: independent estimates of policy liabilities and claims reserves, with ranges rather than point estimates, and a view on how any shortfall flows through to the price.
- Assumption review: mortality, morbidity, lapse, expense and investment return assumptions benchmarked against the target's own experience studies and market data. Lapse and expense assumptions carry most of the sensitivity in long-term savings and protection books.
- Model and data integrity: a check that the valuation model reproduces reported results, that policy data reconciles to the general ledger, and that spreadsheet and model risk controls are adequate.
- Embedded options and guarantees: stochastic or deterministic testing of minimum guarantees, participating fund smoothing and surrender value floors.
- Reinsurance: the credit quality of counterparties, treaty terms that change on a change of control, and whether recoveries are properly captured in the capital requirement.
- Participating funds: the allocation of surplus between policyholders and shareholders, which determines how much of the fund value belongs to the buyer. Singapore's rules on participating fund management constrain distributions.

The deliverable should rank findings by value impact so that the deal team can tell which items are price adjustments, which are indemnity or warranty matters, and which are post-completion integration tasks. ## Where deals go wrong

Experience across insurance transactions in the region points to a short list of recurring failures:

- Valuing the book on the seller's assumptions. Buyers who accept the seller's lapse and expense assumptions inherit the seller's optimism. Independent assumptions should be set from experience data and challenged with sensitivities.
- Ignoring capital release timing. A VIF that assumes capital is released at policy maturity will overstate present value if MAS expects the buffer to be held higher during stress.
- Mixing bases. Combining a market-consistent EV with a traditional-basis new business multiple produces a price that neither basis supports.
- Underweighting the regulatory approval path. A change of control of a licensed insurer requires MAS approval under the Insurance Act, and conditions attached to approval can alter capital and governance assumptions after the price is fixed. See [regulatory licensing](/expertise/regulatory-licensing) for how licensing and approval workstreams interact with a transaction timetable.
- Late discovery of IFRS 17 effects. If the buyer's reporting under SFRS(I) 17 produces a materially different opening CSM, the deal can show an immediate earnings or equity effect that the board did not expect.
- Treating the model as an input rather than evidence. Models that have not been independently peer reviewed are a frequent source of post-completion price disputes.

## Structuring the appraisal for the board

The board and the transaction committee need the appraisal in a form they can challenge. A practical pack contains the EV and appraisal value on a stated basis, a sensitivity table for discount rate, lapse, mortality and expense assumptions, the RBC2 projected solvency position over at least three years after completion, the reconciliation to IFRS 17 equity and CSM, and a list of price adjustments with the supporting evidence.

The range matters as much as the point estimate. A buyer who understands that a 10% rise in lapses removes a defined amount of VIF can negotiate protection, whether through price, contingent consideration or a specific indemnity, rather than discovering the exposure after completion.

Timing also matters. MAS approval, the financial year-end close and the availability of audited valuation results all constrain when data can be refreshed. Build the actuarial timetable backwards from the signing date, and agree a data cut-off date early so that all parties are working from the same position.
