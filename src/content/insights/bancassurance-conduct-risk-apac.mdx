## The Bancassurance Paradox

Bancassurance is simultaneously one of the most commercially significant insurance distribution channels in Asia Pacific and one of the most persistently problematic from a conduct perspective. Banks have access to large customer bases with known financial profiles and trusted advisory relationships. Insurers benefit from that distribution reach at a fraction of the cost of building agent networks. The economics are compelling for both parties.

The conduct record, however, has been poor enough across the region to attract sustained regulatory attention. Mis-selling through bank channels -- customers buying products they did not understand, did not need or that were unsuitable for their circumstances -- has generated complaints, compensation schemes and regulatory action in Singapore, Hong Kong, South Korea, mainland China and other markets. The pattern is consistent enough to suggest structural causes, not isolated failures.

Understanding those structural causes, and how regulation is responding to them, is necessary for any bank or insurer operating in or planning to enter the bancassurance channel in Asia Pacific.

## Why Bancassurance Generates Conduct Risk

The conduct problems in bancassurance do not arise from bad faith in most cases. They arise from structural features of the channel that create misaligned incentives and information asymmetries between the selling party and the customer.

**Sales incentive structures** in bancassurance have historically been weighted toward upfront commission on product sales, with limited adjustment for claims experience, customer retention or suitability outcomes. Bank relationship managers and tellers who receive significant income from insurance product sales face an incentive to recommend products that generate commission rather than products that best meet the customer's needs. The size of that incentive relative to base salary is a strong predictor of conduct outcomes.

**Product complexity** in the life insurance and investment-linked products that are most commonly distributed through bank channels creates information asymmetry between the bank representative and the customer. A customer who does not understand how a product's returns are calculated, what conditions apply to early surrender, or what the product actually covers cannot make an informed purchase decision regardless of whether the representative has disclosed all the required information.

**Sales pressure at the point of transaction** is a feature of bank distribution that is less prevalent in the independent financial adviser channel. A customer who is at the bank for a routine transaction and is offered an insurance product by a trusted relationship manager is in a context that is not conducive to careful consideration of a complex long-term financial commitment.

**Exclusive distribution agreements** create additional tension. Where a bank has an exclusive arrangement with a single insurance partner, the bank's representatives are constrained in the recommendations they can make regardless of whether another product might better suit the customer. The conflict between the distribution economics and the customer's interest is structural.

## The Regulatory Response in Singapore

MAS has been one of the more proactive regulators in Asia Pacific in addressing bancassurance conduct. The regulatory framework has developed through a combination of conduct rules, product governance requirements and supervisory action.

**Notice FAA-N16 and the MAS Financial Advisers Act framework** apply to banks and their representatives when they distribute insurance products in Singapore. The suitability assessment requirements, disclosure obligations and documentation standards under this framework are substantive and have been a focus of MAS supervisory attention.

**MAS's balanced scorecard requirements** for financial advisers and their representatives were introduced specifically to address commission-driven mis-selling. The balanced scorecard requires that representative remuneration incorporates quality metrics -- customer outcomes, complaint rates, recission rates -- alongside sales volume. The effect has been to make conduct-related outcomes a direct input to remuneration rather than a separate compliance matter.

**Product due diligence obligations** on distributors have been strengthened. Banks distributing insurance products are expected to conduct genuine due diligence on the products they distribute, including assessing whether products are fit for the customer segments they will be distributed to and whether the product governance process of the insurer meets the expected standard.

**MAS reviews and enforcement actions** in the bancassurance space have covered both system-level thematic issues and individual firm actions. The remediation requirements from these reviews -- including customer remediation schemes, systems and controls enhancements and management accountability measures -- have shaped practice across the industry.

## Regional Regulatory Developments

**Hong Kong** experienced significant bancassurance mis-selling issues in the context of complex investment and insurance products. The Insurance Authority has enhanced its conduct requirements for insurance intermediaries, including bank-based representatives, and has introduced individual licensing of insurance intermediaries that increases personal accountability.

**Vietnam** has been the subject of sustained media and regulatory attention on bancassurance conduct following widespread complaints about mis-selling of investment-linked life insurance through bank channels. The response has included enhanced cooling-off period requirements, stricter suitability assessment obligations and restrictions on how bancassurance products can be marketed in bank branches.

**South Korea and mainland China** have both taken enforcement actions against banks and insurers following bancassurance mis-selling, with financial penalties and requirements to compensate affected customers. The scale of these actions reflects the scale of the distribution channel in both markets.

**Indonesia and Malaysia** are at varying stages of developing enhanced bancassurance conduct frameworks, with increasing regulatory attention on the information asymmetries and incentive structures that have generated problems elsewhere.

## What Good Bancassurance Governance Looks Like

For banks and insurers that are committed to operating the bancassurance channel to a high conduct standard, the governance requirements are clear even where the regulatory framework does not yet mandate them.

**Product governance** starts with the insurer but extends to the bank as distributor. A product that is suitable for some customer segments may not be suitable for others, and the distribution agreement should clearly define the target market and the sales process appropriate for it. Banks should have a formal product approval process for new products they agree to distribute, not simply accept what the insurance partner proposes.

**Representative training and competency** needs to go beyond the minimum required for licensing. Representatives distributing life insurance and investment-linked products need to genuinely understand what they are selling, including the terms and conditions that matter most to customer outcomes -- surrender values, exclusions, return mechanics. Testing that competency, not just training and ticking a box, should be standard.

**Suitability assessment in practice** -- not as a documentation exercise but as a genuine process of understanding the customer's financial position, risk tolerance and insurance needs before making a recommendation -- is the core conduct requirement. The documentation of that assessment should reflect what actually happened in the conversation, not a template that is completed after the sale.

**Remuneration structures** that balance sales incentives with quality metrics reduce the structural misalignment that drives mis-selling. Implementing the MAS balanced scorecard approach, even in markets where it is not yet required, is a way to demonstrate commitment to conduct standards and to reduce the regulatory risk inherent in the channel.

**Customer outcome monitoring** after the point of sale -- tracking complaint rates, recission rates, claims experience and lapse rates by product and by representative -- provides the management information needed to identify conduct issues before they become regulatory problems.
