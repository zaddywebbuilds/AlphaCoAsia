24:T2a36,<p>An insurance regulatory consultant in Singapore is the better choice when the work is operational and technical: building a framework, preparing a licence application, drafting an ORSA, remediating a compliance gap or supporting a Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) inspection response. A law firm is the better choice when the work needs a legal opinion, contractual drafting, privilege or representation in an enforcement matter. Most insurers need both at different points, and the practical question is how to divide the work so that neither adviser duplicates the other or leaves a gap between them.</p>
<p>This guide sets out where the two roles differ, which regulatory workstreams belong with each, and how to scope a combined engagement for a licensed insurer, reinsurer, captive or applicant.</p>
<h2>What each adviser is built to do</h2>
<p>A law firm interprets legal text and applies it to facts. Its core outputs are legal opinions, transaction documents, regulatory submissions that carry legal characterisation, and advocacy. Where the question is what the Insurance Act 1966, the Financial Advisers Act 2001 or a subsidiary regulation requires, or whether a proposed structure falls on the right side of a legal boundary, counsel is the right source.</p>
<p>A regulatory consultant translates regulatory expectations into working frameworks, documents and processes. The question is less what the rule says and more how the rule is met in an insurer that has a particular size, product mix and operating model. Typical outputs include gap assessments against MAS notices and guidelines, policy and procedure suites, board and committee papers, risk registers, independent reviews and licence application packages.</p>
<p>The two overlap on interpretation, which is where scoping errors start. A lawyer will often interpret a requirement correctly but stop short of designing the control that meets it. A consultant will design the control but should not be relied on for a binding legal view. Treating the boundary as explicit avoids both outcomes.</p>
<h2>Workstreams that usually suit a regulatory consultant</h2>
<p>Several recurring insurer workstreams are mainly operational and technical, and sit comfortably with a specialist adviser:</p>
<ul>
<li>Licence applications and licence variations, where the heavy lifting is the business plan, capital and solvency projections, governance structure, key appointment holder documentation and policy suite. The <a href="/expertise/regulatory-licensing">regulatory licensing</a> process is document intensive and sequence driven.</li>
<li>ORSA design and review, including risk appetite, stress and reverse stress scenarios, and the board narrative. See <a href="/expertise/orsa-advisory">ORSA advisory</a>.</li>
<li>Enterprise risk management framework reviews against MAS Notice 126 and the related guidelines on risk management practices. See <a href="/expertise/enterprise-risk-management">enterprise risk management</a>.</li>
<li>AML/CFT programme build and remediation for life insurers under MAS Notice 314, covering risk assessment, customer due diligence procedures, screening and suspicious transaction reporting workflows. See <a href="/expertise/aml-cft">AML/CFT</a>.</li>
<li>Independent reviews and pre-inspection readiness assessments, where the value lies in an objective benchmark of current practice against supervisory expectations.</li>
<li>RBC2 readiness and capital analysis, where the output is a model, a data lineage or a reconciliation rather than a legal document. See <a href="/expertise/risk-based-capital">risk-based capital</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>In each case the deliverable is something the insurer will operate, not merely read. That is the practical test for whether a consultant fits.</p>
<h2>Workstreams that usually suit a law firm</h2>
<p>Other workstreams depend on legal analysis, privilege or enforceability, and should be led by counsel:</p>
<ul>
<li>Legal opinions on whether an activity is regulated, whether a licence or exemption is needed, or how a cross-border arrangement is characterised.</li>
<li>Share purchase agreements, reinsurance treaties, outsourcing agreements and other contracts where drafting precision and enforceability matter.</li>
<li>Approvals involving ownership and control, including the controller approval process under the Insurance Act, where legal structuring of the acquirer or shareholder chain is central.</li>
<li>Enforcement, composition or investigation matters, where legal representation and the handling of privileged material are the priority.</li>
<li>Disputes, policyholder litigation and complaints that escalate beyond internal resolution.</li>
<li>Corporate secretarial and constitutional matters, such as changes to constitutional documents and shareholder arrangements.</li>
</ul>
<p>Privilege deserves specific attention. Legal professional privilege generally attaches to communications with lawyers for the purpose of legal advice, not to advice from a non-lawyer consultant. If an insurer expects to need a candid assessment of an actual or potential breach, it should confirm with counsel before commissioning that assessment, and consider whether counsel should instruct the consultant. This is a point for the insurer's lawyers to decide, not for the consultant.</p>
<h2>Where the two roles genuinely overlap</h2>
<p>Some matters need both advisers working from a shared plan. Three are common.</p>
<p>The first is a new licence application. Counsel advises on the legal basis for the proposed licence class, the shareholder structure and any exemptions. The consultant builds the business plan, governance framework, risk management and compliance policies, financial projections and the application narrative. MAS has published indicative processing timelines for different licence types, but those assume a complete and coherent submission, so the legal and technical streams must be reconciled before filing.</p>
<p>The second is a regulatory breach or supervisory finding. Counsel advises on exposure, notification obligations and communications with MAS. The consultant designs the remediation plan, tests the controls and produces evidence that the fix is operating. MAS generally expects a credible root cause analysis and a time-bound remediation plan, which is operational work.</p>
<p>The third is a group restructuring or acquisition. Counsel handles the transaction documents and approvals. The consultant supports due diligence on the target's risk, compliance and capital position, and plans the integration of governance and reporting. See <a href="/expertise/due-diligence-corporate-deals">due diligence and corporate deals</a>.</p>
<h2>How to decide: five practical questions</h2>
<p>When a new workstream arises, five questions usually settle the choice:</p>
<ol>
<li>Is the primary output a legal opinion, a contract or a representation? If yes, start with a law firm.</li>
<li>Is the primary output a framework, model, procedure or report that the insurer will operate? If yes, start with a consultant.</li>
<li>Does the matter involve potential enforcement or litigation? If yes, involve counsel first and let counsel decide who else is instructed.</li>
<li>Does the work depend on technical subject knowledge, such as actuarial, risk or compliance operations, that a general legal practice may not hold? If yes, bring in a specialist.</li>
<li>Will MAS or an auditor test the output in practice? If yes, the deliverable needs to be operable and evidenced, which favours a practitioner.</li>
</ol>
<p>If the answers point in both directions, the work is a combined engagement.</p>
<h2>Scoping a combined engagement</h2>
<p>A combined engagement works best when the insurer owns the integration. Several habits help.</p>
<p>Name one internal owner, typically the Chief Risk Officer, Head of Compliance or Chief Financial Officer, who reconciles the advisers' outputs. External advisers should not be left to coordinate with each other without a decision maker.</p>
<p>Write down the boundary. Each engagement letter should say which questions the adviser will and will not answer, and who relies on the output. A consultant's engagement letter should state that it does not provide legal advice, and a law firm's should state that operational design is outside scope.</p>
<p>Agree sequencing. Legal characterisation often needs to settle before detailed framework design, and framework design often needs to settle before the final submission is reviewed by counsel. A shared timeline with explicit hand-offs prevents rework.</p>
<p>Agree how documents are shared. If privilege is a concern, route sensitive material through counsel and keep the consultant's working papers separate where appropriate.</p>
<p>Check independence where it matters. For independent reviews, the reviewer should not have designed the framework being reviewed, and the insurer should record that in its governance papers.</p>
<h2>Cost and speed considerations</h2>
<p>Law firm rates for senior time are generally higher than specialist consultancy rates, so using counsel for operational drafting can be an inefficient allocation. Conversely, using a consultant for a question that needs a legal opinion creates risk that no fee saving justifies. The most economical pattern is to use counsel for the narrow legal questions and the consultant for the volume of operational work, with a short, written handover between them.</p>
<p>Speed also differs. Regulatory deadlines, such as an inspection response, a licence variation window or a remediation commitment to MAS, often favour a team that can mobilise practitioners who have built the relevant documents before. Insurers should ask any prospective adviser which comparable pieces of work its named team members have personally delivered, and should confirm the staffing before signing.</p>
<h2>Selecting a regulatory consultant</h2>
<p>When evaluating an insurance regulatory consultant in Singapore, insurers should look for subject-matter depth in the specific regime involved, whether licensing, ERM and ORSA, RBC2, AML/CFT or actuarial work; named senior practitioners rather than a bench of unnamed juniors; a clear statement of what is out of scope; and deliverables written to the standard that MAS reviewers and auditors will test. References from comparable licensed institutions are worth requesting, subject to the adviser's confidentiality obligations.</p>
