Digital Distribution and Conduct Risk: What Insurers and Intermediaries Need to Know

The growth of digital insurance distribution has moved faster than regulatory frameworks across most Asia Pacific markets. Regulators are catching up, with conduct risk, suitability assessment and product governance all under increased scrutiny. The scale of digital distribution amplifies conduct exposure rather than reducing the standard of care expected. We examine the key obligations and practical controls insurers and digital platforms should have in place.

The Conduct Gap in Digital Insurance

Digital insurance distribution has grown faster than almost any other area of the insurance market in Asia Pacific. Aggregator platforms, embedded insurance in e-commerce and fintech apps, direct-to-consumer digital journeys, and bancassurance delivered through mobile banking interfaces have all expanded the reach of insurance to segments and geographies that were previously underserved.

The speed of that expansion has created a conduct gap. The regulatory frameworks governing how insurance is sold, what disclosures are required, and how suitable products are matched to customers were built around face-to-face or telephone distribution. The conduct obligations they contain do not disappear when a human adviser is replaced by a digital journey. In many respects, the stakes are higher: digital distribution operates at scale, which means that a systematic conduct failure affects many more customers than an individual adviser's poor practice would.

Regulators across Asia Pacific have been working to address this. MAS has been the most active, publishing guidance on digital advisory services, robo-advice frameworks and the application of conduct standards to digital channels. Other regulators are following. For insurers and digital platforms, understanding where the conduct exposure sits and having adequate controls in place is now a supervisory expectation, not an optional standard.

The Distribution Models Under Scrutiny

Digital insurance distribution takes several forms, and the regulatory treatment differs between them.

Direct-to-consumer digital journeys where the insurer sells directly to the customer without an intermediary are the simplest model from a licensing perspective. The insurer carries the full conduct responsibility: product design, disclosure, suitability assessment where required, and claims handling. The digital channel does not reduce any of these obligations.

Aggregators and comparison platforms present a more complex conduct picture. Aggregators that display and compare products but do not advise on them may operate under different licensing requirements than those that make recommendations. The distinction between providing information and providing advice is a regulatory bright line in Singapore and most regional markets, and digital platforms that have moved from one side to the other without updating their regulatory status face exposure.

Embedded insurance distributed through non-insurance digital platforms, such as travel insurance sold through a booking app or device protection sold through a telco, introduces the question of where conduct responsibility sits. The insurer cannot fully discharge its conduct obligations by contracting with a distribution partner. Regulatory expectations in most markets require the insurer to maintain oversight of how its products are presented and sold through third-party channels.

Bancassurance delivered digitally is increasingly common as banks move customers to digital channels for their banking needs and extend that to insurance. Banks are regulated financial institutions with their own conduct obligations, but those do not fully substitute for the insurer's responsibilities, and the regulatory treatment of the distribution arrangement itself requires careful attention.

Suitability Assessment in a Digital Journey

Suitability assessment is one of the most challenging conduct obligations to implement well in a digital context. The obligation exists because insurance products, particularly those with investment components, can cause significant financial harm if they are purchased by customers for whom they are not appropriate.

In a face-to-face setting, suitability assessment involves a trained adviser understanding the customer's financial situation, needs and objectives, and matching them to a product. In a digital journey, the equivalent process must be built into the design of the customer experience.

The regulatory expectation is not that digital platforms are exempt from suitability requirements. It is that those requirements are met through the design of the digital journey. This means:

  • Fact-finding that captures sufficient information about the customer's financial position, risk tolerance and insurance needs to support a suitability determination
  • Logic that applies the gathered information to the available products and either recommends suitable options or excludes unsuitable ones
  • Clear disclosure to the customer of what the assessment covers and what it does not
  • Documentation of the suitability process that can be reviewed in a supervision context

Where a digital journey involves a recommendation, most regional regulators treat that as regulated advice. Where it does not involve a recommendation and the customer self-selects from a range of options, the obligation typically shifts to disclosure: the platform must provide adequate information for the customer to make an informed choice, and must not present that information in a misleading way.

The boundary between recommendation and information-only presentation is an area of active regulatory development. Digital platforms should have legal and compliance input on how their customer journey is characterised and should monitor regulatory guidance as it develops.

Product Governance and Target Market Determination

Product governance requirements expect insurers to design products with a defined target market in mind, and to ensure that distribution arrangements reach the intended market effectively.

Target market determination requires the insurer to identify, before a product is launched, the customer segment for which the product is designed. This should consider the product features, the price and fees, the risks to the customer of buying the product, and the distribution channel through which it will be sold. A product with complex features that carry significant financial risk is not appropriate for all segments, and the target market determination should reflect this.

Distribution alignment is the obligation that follows. The insurer must ensure that the distribution channels used reach the intended target market and do not systematically sell the product to customers outside that market. In digital distribution, where the scale is large and the individual customer interaction is limited, monitoring the distribution outcome against the target market determination is both more important and more technically feasible than in traditional channels, given the data available.

Regular review of both the product design and the distribution outcome is expected. If sales data shows systematic patterns inconsistent with the target market, such as high lapse rates in a particular customer segment or a concentration of complaints from a particular age group or distribution channel, this should trigger a review of whether the product or the distribution arrangement needs to change.

Claims Handling Transparency and Complaints

Conduct risk in digital insurance does not end at the point of sale. The claims experience is a critical part of the customer journey, and it is increasingly a focus of supervisory scrutiny across the region.

Claims turnaround times are monitored by regulators. Unreasonable delays in processing or paying legitimate claims cause customer harm and attract supervisory attention. Digital distribution that creates efficient customer acquisition but slow claims handling is a pattern that regulators have specifically identified as a conduct concern.

Claims denial rates and patterns are another indicator. A high proportion of claims denied for technical policy exclusions that customers were unlikely to have understood at the point of sale is a signal that the product disclosure was inadequate. This is particularly relevant for short-term policies sold through digital channels where policy terms are often not read by customers before purchase.

Complaints data should be treated as a conduct management tool, not just a service metric. The pattern of complaints, including the reasons for complaints and the resolution outcome, provides insight into where the customer journey has failed. Systematic complaints about a particular feature, coverage gap or disclosure issue should prompt a root cause analysis and remediation.

Digital channels must provide accessible complaints mechanisms. A customer who bought a policy digitally should be able to raise a complaint through the same channel, or through an accessible alternative. Making complaints difficult is itself a conduct concern.

Practical Controls for Insurers and Digital Platforms

The conduct framework for digital insurance does not need to be more complex than necessary, but it does need to address the specific risks that digital distribution creates.

Customer journey review. Document the full digital customer journey, from first awareness through to post-sale service, and identify at each step where conduct obligations apply and whether they are being met. This is the foundation on which more detailed controls can be built.

Disclosure testing. Test how customers actually interact with your disclosure materials. Do they read them? Do they understand them? This can be assessed through user research, A/B testing and analysis of the questions and complaints you receive after purchase. Disclosure that is legally complete but practically unread is not meeting the conduct standard.

Monitoring and MI. Build systematic monitoring of the key conduct metrics: suitability assessment completion rates, policy cancellation patterns, claims ratios by product and channel, complaints volume and reasons, and resolution outcomes. These should be reviewed regularly by the compliance function and reported to senior management.

Intermediary oversight framework. If you distribute through third-party digital platforms or embed your products in another company's app, document your oversight framework for those arrangements. What conduct standards do you require your distribution partners to meet? How do you verify compliance? What happens when you identify a problem?

Staff training. Even in a highly automated digital distribution model, people make decisions about how the journey is designed, what disclosures are shown, and how claims are handled. Those people need to understand the conduct obligations their decisions create.

Regulatory change monitoring. The regulatory framework for digital distribution in Asia Pacific is actively developing. MAS, the Hong Kong Insurance Authority, BNM and other regional regulators are all publishing guidance and updating rules. Maintaining a systematic approach to monitoring and assessing the impact of regulatory change on your digital distribution model is essential.
