18:T2602,<p>The MAS fit and proper assessment is the test the Monetary Authority of Singapore applies to the people who control and manage a licensed insurer, and it is one of the most common causes of delay in licensing and board change projects. MAS sets out its approach in the Guidelines on Fit and Proper Criteria (FSG-G01), which apply across financial institutions and are read together with the Insurance Act 1966 and its subsidiary legislation. For an insurer, a weak submission on a single director or chief executive can hold up an entire licence application or a planned management change.</p>
<p>This article sets out who is assessed, the criteria MAS applies, what a complete submission contains and where applicants most often run into difficulty.</p>
<h2>Who Is Subject to the Fit and Proper Assessment</h2>
<p>The assessment is not limited to the board. In practice MAS looks at several groups of persons connected to an insurer.</p>
<h3>Directors and key appointment holders</h3>
<p>Directors, the chief executive officer, the deputy chief executive officer, the chief financial officer, the chief risk officer, the head of compliance, the head of internal audit and the appointed actuary are treated as key appointments. The Insurance Act 1966 and the Insurance (Corporate Governance) Regulations 2013 require insurers to obtain MAS approval before certain appointments take effect, and to notify MAS of others. Insurers should confirm the current list of approval and notification triggers against the Act, the regulations and the relevant MAS notices before each appointment, rather than relying on an internal checklist that may be out of date.</p>
<h3>Controllers and shareholders</h3>
<p>MAS also assesses the fitness and propriety of substantial shareholders and controllers. Where a change in control is proposed, the acquirer and its own ultimate owners and directors come under review. The same four criteria apply to them, with added attention to source of funds and the soundness of the acquirer's own group.</p>
<h3>Persons appointed after licensing</h3>
<p>The obligation does not end at licensing. Every later board appointment, executive hire or shareholder change re-opens the question, and MAS expects the insurer itself to run an internal assessment before it submits anything.</p>
<h2>The Four Criteria MAS Applies</h2>
<p>FSG-G01 organises the assessment around four criteria. MAS has said it takes a holistic view, so a shortfall on one criterion does not automatically disqualify a person, but it does require a clear explanation.</p>
<h3>Honesty, integrity and reputation</h3>
<p>MAS considers whether the person has been convicted of an offence involving fraud, dishonesty or breach of trust, whether they have been subject to regulatory or disciplinary action in any jurisdiction, and whether they have been involved in a failed or poorly managed business. Pending proceedings and past employment terminations for cause are also relevant.</p>
<h3>Competence and capability</h3>
<p>For an insurer, MAS looks for relevant knowledge and experience in the specific role. A candidate for chief risk officer is expected to show experience with insurance risk, capital and the enterprise risk management framework. A candidate for the board needs enough understanding of the business model to challenge management. Seniority in another industry does not substitute for this.</p>
<h3>Financial soundness</h3>
<p>MAS considers whether the person has been declared bankrupt, has outstanding judgment debts, or has been involved in insolvency proceedings. Personal financial difficulty can bear on how a person manages conflicts and pressure in a control function.</p>
<h3>Time commitment and conflicts</h3>
<p>Outside directorships, executive roles elsewhere and group responsibilities all affect whether a person can discharge their duties. MAS also tests independence where the role requires it, which links to the board composition requirements in the Insurance (Corporate Governance) Regulations 2013.</p>
<h2>Preparing a Complete Submission</h2>
<p>Most delays arise from incomplete or inconsistent documents rather than from adverse findings. A well-prepared submission generally includes the following.</p>
<ul>
<li>A completed MAS application form for the appointment, signed by the insurer and the individual.</li>
<li>A curriculum vitae with full employment history and no unexplained gaps.</li>
<li>A role description and the insurer's own written assessment of why the person meets each criterion.</li>
<li>Declarations covering criminal, civil, regulatory and financial history, supported by documents where an event is disclosed.</li>
<li>Evidence of qualifications and professional memberships, such as actuarial credentials for an appointed actuary.</li>
<li>A schedule of other directorships and commitments, with time estimates.</li>
</ul>
<p>Consistency across documents matters. If the curriculum vitae, the form and a prior filing in another jurisdiction describe the same role differently, MAS will ask, and each clarification round adds time.</p>
<h2>Common Reasons for Delay</h2>
<p>Across licensing and board change projects, a small number of issues recur. Understanding them helps the insurer test its own file before submission.</p>
<ul>
<li>Role descriptions that are generic and do not show how the person's experience maps to insurance risk, capital or actuarial matters.</li>
<li>Unexplained gaps in employment history, or overlapping roles that raise questions about time commitment.</li>
<li>Declarations that do not match public records, such as court or regulatory databases in other jurisdictions.</li>
<li>Referees who cannot speak to the person's conduct in a comparable regulated role.</li>
<li>Late disclosure of a new matter between submission and approval, which obliges the insurer to update MAS and restarts part of the review.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Handling Adverse Disclosures</h2>
<p>A disclosure does not end an application, but how it is presented does influence the outcome. Where a person has a past regulatory finding, a dismissed claim or a business failure, the insurer should address it directly in the submission.</p>
<h3>What a useful explanation contains</h3>
<p>A useful explanation states the facts, the outcome, the time elapsed, what the person has done since and why the insurer is satisfied that the matter does not affect the role. Supporting documents such as court orders, regulator letters or references from independent parties should be attached rather than offered on request.</p>
<h3>What to avoid</h3>
<p>Minimising or omitting an event is the most damaging approach. MAS will often find it through its own enquiries or through information shared with overseas regulators, and an omission becomes an honesty concern in its own right. The insurer's own accountability also comes into question, because the insurer is expected to have carried out due diligence on the candidate.</p>
<h2>Timeline and Process Considerations</h2>
<p>Timing depends on the completeness of the file and whether the appointment forms part of a wider application. For standalone appointments, insurers should build in enough time for MAS to raise questions and for the individual to respond, and should avoid fixing a start date before approval is in hand. For a new licence application, the fit and proper submissions for all proposed directors and key appointment holders are reviewed with the business plan, so a single late or incomplete submission can hold back the whole application. We discuss the wider application sequence in our article on <a href="/insights/preparing-mas-insurance-licence-application">preparing a MAS insurance licence application</a>.</p>
<p>Insurers should also plan for succession. If a key appointment holder leaves unexpectedly, the insurer needs a credible interim arrangement and a candidate whose file is ready to submit. Boards that keep pre-assessed candidates for critical roles are in a stronger position than those that begin the process after a resignation.</p>
<h2>Embedding the Assessment in Governance</h2>
<p>MAS expects fit and proper to be a continuing obligation, not a one-time test. Practical steps for the board and nominating committee include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Adopting a written fit and proper policy that covers initial assessment, annual re-confirmation and the events that trigger reassessment.</li>
<li>Requiring directors and key appointment holders to declare changes in circumstances promptly, including investigations, litigation and new external roles.</li>
<li>Keeping assessment records that show the evidence reviewed and the reasoning, so the insurer can demonstrate its process during a MAS inspection.</li>
<li>Running periodic board skills reviews that test whether collective competence still matches the insurer's business, including areas such as technology risk and capital management.</li>
</ul>
<p>Where the insurer is part of a group, local assessment should not rely solely on a group-level process. MAS expects the Singapore entity to be able to show its own judgement and to retain the records locally.</p>
