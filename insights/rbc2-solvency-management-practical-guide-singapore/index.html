Singapore RBC2 Capital Requirements: A Practical Guide to Solvency Management

A practical guide to managing RBC2 capital requirements in Singapore beyond framework implementation. Covers internal capital targets, tiered solvency buffers, sensitivity management, ORSA alignment and optimisation levers under MAS Notice 133.

RBC2 capital requirements in Singapore are managed well when the insurer treats the regulatory solvency ratio as a planning variable rather than a quarterly output: set an internal target above the regulatory floor, project the ratio forward under stress, and agree in advance which management actions are triggered at each level. This guide covers the day-to-day capital management that follows once the framework is implemented, rather than the framework itself, which we address in our overview of the RBC2 capital framework.

The regulatory baseline under Notice 133

The Risk-Based Capital 2 framework (RBC2) applies to insurers licensed under the Insurance Act 1966. Its detailed requirements sit in the Insurance (Valuation and Capital) Regulations and in MAS Notice 133, the Notice on Valuation and Capital Framework for Insurers. Together they define how insurance liabilities are valued, which assets and instruments qualify as capital, and how the Total Risk Requirement is calculated.

The ratio most management teams track is the Capital Adequacy Ratio, which compares Financial Resources with the Total Risk Requirement. Financial Resources are made up of Available Capital after the adjustments and limits MAS prescribes, and the Total Risk Requirement reflects the insurance, market, credit, operational and other risk charges calibrated by MAS. The statutory minimums and the thresholds at which MAS expects engagement are set out in the regulations and in the relevant MAS notices; always confirm the current figures against the instruments in force rather than against internal summaries that may predate an amendment.

Two practical points follow. First, RBC2 is a fund-based framework for insurers with separate insurance funds, so the ratio has to be understood fund by fund as well as at the entity level. Second, the position reported to MAS is a point in time. The commercial question is what the ratio will look like after the next new business cycle, the next market move and the next dividend decision.

Setting an internal capital target and solvency buffer

Holding capital exactly at the regulatory requirement leaves no room for volatility. Most insurers therefore adopt an internal target, expressed as a Capital Adequacy Ratio, that sits above the regulatory requirement and is approved by the board as part of the risk appetite statement.

A defensible target is built from the insurer's own risk profile, not from a peer benchmark. The inputs usually include:

  • The volatility of the ratio under the insurer's own stress scenarios, particularly equity, interest rate and credit spread shocks.
  • The speed at which capital can be raised or released, for example through a parent injection, reinsurance or a change in asset mix.
  • Concentrations that the standard formula may not fully reflect, such as a single large reinsurer or a heavily weighted investment strategy.
  • The insurer's commitments to policyholders, rating agencies and group parents, which can be more demanding than the regulatory minimum.

The target should be tiered. A common structure uses a target level at which the insurer operates normally, an early warning level at which the Chief Risk Officer escalates to the board risk committee, and a trigger level at which pre-agreed management actions begin. Each tier should have an owner, a defined escalation path and a documented list of actions. Our work on risk-based capital often starts with exactly this: converting a single ratio target into a governed set of thresholds.

Linking RBC2 to the ORSA

MAS Notice 126 requires licensed insurers to maintain an enterprise risk management framework, which includes the Own Risk and Solvency Assessment. The ORSA is where the internal capital target is justified and tested. It should show, over the business planning horizon, how the ratio evolves under the base case and under adverse scenarios, and whether the insurer remains above its internal and regulatory thresholds.

Where capital management goes wrong, the ORSA and the capital plan are produced by different teams on different assumptions. The ORSA projects one new business volume, the capital plan another, and the dividend assumption in the business plan is not the one in the capital projection. A single set of assumptions, owned by finance and actuarial and reviewed by risk, removes this gap. For further detail on the assessment itself, see our guidance on ORSA advisory.

Sensitivity management: understanding what moves the ratio

Capital management depends on knowing which exposures move the ratio most. Under RBC2 the ratio responds to both the numerator and the denominator, and a market move can shift both at once. A fall in interest rates can raise the value of liabilities and, depending on duration matching, change the value of assets by a different amount, while also changing the market risk charge.

We recommend that the actuarial function maintains a standing sensitivity table, refreshed at least quarterly, covering:

  • Parallel and non-parallel interest rate movements, up and down.
  • Equity and property value shocks.
  • Credit spread widening and rating migration on the bond portfolio.
  • Changes in lapse, mortality, morbidity or claims assumptions, depending on the business.
  • The loss of a reinsurance arrangement or a reinsurer downgrade.

The table is more useful when it is expressed in the same units the board uses to make decisions: the change in the Capital Adequacy Ratio and the change in surplus above the internal target. A sensitivity that is reported only in Singapore dollar terms is hard for directors to compare against appetite.

Optimisation levers within the rules

RBC optimisation should be understood as improving capital efficiency inside the framework, not as engineering the reported ratio. The levers available to most insurers fall into four groups.

Asset allocation. Market and credit risk charges differ by asset class, rating and duration, and asset-liability duration mismatch affects both charges and the valuation of liabilities. Changing the allocation can improve capital efficiency, but it also changes yield, liquidity and concentration, so the effect on return and risk appetite has to be assessed alongside the effect on the Total Risk Requirement.

Reinsurance. Reinsurance can reduce insurance risk charges, but the recognition of reinsurance, the credit risk charge for reinsurance counterparties and any concentration limits all matter. The structure should be modelled on the RBC2 basis before it is placed. A treaty that looks efficient on an economic basis can be less so once counterparty charges are included. Our note on reinsurance regulatory requirements sets out the regulatory considerations.

Product mix and pricing. The capital consumption of new business differs by product. Embedding RBC2 capital cost in product pricing and profit testing means the insurer knows the return on capital of each line before it commits to growth. This is particularly relevant for products with guarantees or long-dated liabilities.

Capital instruments and structure. Whether a subordinated instrument or a parent capital injection qualifies as capital, and in which tier, depends on the criteria in Notice 133 and the regulations. Instruments should be designed against those criteria from the outset, and MAS engagement should happen before issuance rather than after.

Governance, data and the role of the actuarial function

Capital management depends on data and model governance as much as on technique. Valuation and capital figures reported under Notice 133 flow from policy data, asset data and actuarial models. Weak controls over any of these will surface as restatements, late submissions or unexplained movements in the ratio.

Practitioners should establish clear model ownership, version control and documented validation for the models that feed the ratio. Material assumptions, such as lapse and expense assumptions, should have an owner, a review cycle and a record of approval. The appointed actuary's responsibilities in this area are covered in our article on appointed actuary requirements in Singapore.

Peer review is an effective safeguard where models are complex or where a change in methodology moves the ratio materially. An independent actuarial review gives the board assurance that a movement in the ratio reflects economics and not a model change. See our actuarial consulting page for how such reviews are typically structured.

A quarterly capital management routine

For compliance officers, CFOs and chief risk officers looking for a working rhythm, the following sequence is practical.

Step one: close the reporting position, reconcile it to the Notice 133 submission and explain the movement from the prior period in terms of new business, market movements, assumption changes and management actions.

Step two: refresh the sensitivity table and compare the results with the internal target, early warning level and trigger level.

Step three: update the forward projection used in the business plan and the ORSA, using the same assumptions, and check headroom over the planning horizon.

Step four: report to the board risk committee, including any breach of an internal threshold, the actions taken and the actions proposed.

Step five: record open issues, such as data quality, model limitations or pending regulatory changes, and assign owners and dates.

This routine does not require new systems, only agreed responsibilities and a calendar. Its value is that the first time the board hears about a falling ratio is not after the ratio has fallen.

Common weaknesses we see in solvency management

Several weaknesses recur in the Singapore market. Internal targets are set once and not revisited when the business mix changes. Early warning levels exist on paper but have no assigned actions. Projections assume that capital can be raised on demand, without testing whether a parent or the market would in fact provide it. Management actions credited in stress scenarios are not reconciled with the governance needed to execute them in time. Each of these can be corrected without changing the capital model, which is why they are worth addressing before any technical optimisation.
