17:T2704,<h2>Why Licence Applications Fail or Stall</h2>
<p>Most insurance licence applications that fail or stall do not fail because the applicant does not meet the substantive requirements. They fail because the application is incomplete, the business plan is not credible, the key individuals have not been properly assessed, the governance frameworks are too generic, or the regulatory capital projections have not been prepared to the standard MAS expects.</p>
<p>MAS reviews licence applications carefully and will raise queries where it has questions about the completeness or credibility of the submission. Each round of queries and responses extends the timeline. Applications that require multiple query rounds can take significantly longer than a well-prepared submission.</p>
<p>This article explains what a MAS insurance licence application involves, what MAS will scrutinise closely, and how to give your application the best chance of a smooth process.</p>
<h2>The Types of Insurance Licence Available in Singapore</h2>
<p>MAS issues insurance licences under the Insurance Act. The main licence categories for insurers are:</p>
<p><strong>Direct life insurer</strong> -- for companies writing life insurance business in Singapore.</p>
<p><strong>Direct general insurer</strong> -- for companies writing general insurance business in Singapore.</p>
<p><strong>Composite insurer</strong> -- for companies writing both life and general insurance business.</p>
<p><strong>Captive insurer</strong> -- for companies writing insurance business for the risks of their parent group.</p>
<p><strong>Specialist insurer</strong> -- for companies writing specific classes of business such as marine, aviation or financial risk.</p>
<p><strong>Reinsurer</strong> -- for companies writing reinsurance business in Singapore, including life and general reinsurers.</p>
<p>Each licence category has different capital requirements, governance standards and business plan expectations. The structure of the applicant entity -- branch versus subsidiary, foreign-owned versus domestically-owned -- also affects the requirements.</p>
<h2>What MAS Assesses in a Licence Application</h2>
<p>MAS's assessment of an insurance licence application covers four main areas.</p>
<p><strong>Fitness and propriety of key individuals.</strong> MAS will assess the fitness and propriety of the proposed directors, the chief executive and other key management personnel. This involves background checks, assessment of relevant experience and qualifications, and in some cases meetings with MAS. Key individuals need to demonstrate relevant experience for the roles they will hold and to show that they have not been involved in conduct incompatible with holding a senior position in a regulated entity.</p>
<p><strong>Soundness of the business plan.</strong> The business plan needs to demonstrate that the proposed insurance business is commercially viable, that the applicant understands the market it intends to enter, and that the revenue and expense projections are realistic. A business plan that is vague about how the insurer will acquire customers, how it will build distribution and how it will compete with established players will not be persuasive. MAS will also assess whether the proposed products and business model are appropriate for a newly licensed entity.</p>
<p><strong>Adequacy of governance and risk management frameworks.</strong> The application must demonstrate that the insurer will be governed to an appropriate standard. This includes the governance structure -- board composition, board committees, management authority -- and the risk management framework, including how the insurer will manage its exposure to underwriting risk, market risk, credit risk and operational risk. The frameworks submitted with the application should be genuinely tailored to the insurer's proposed business, not generic templates with the company name inserted.</p>
<p><strong>Financial strength and capital adequacy.</strong> The applicant must demonstrate that it has adequate capital to support the proposed business under the RBC2 framework and that the projected capital position will be maintained at a satisfactory level as the business grows. The financial projections should include a credible base case and stress scenarios that show the capital position remains adequate under adverse conditions.</p>
<h2>Common Reasons Applications Are Delayed</h2>
<p><strong>Incomplete or generic governance frameworks.</strong> Submitting AML/CFT policies, risk management frameworks and compliance manuals that are clearly templates rather than frameworks designed for the specific business is a common reason for MAS queries. MAS expects to see frameworks that reflect the actual nature and risks of the proposed business.</p>
<p><strong>Unrealistic financial projections.</strong> Business plans with financial projections that are either very optimistic about revenue growth or that do not adequately account for the costs of building a licensed insurer from scratch will attract scrutiny. MAS will compare the projections against the proposed business model and may ask for the assumptions underlying the numbers to be justified.</p>
<p><strong>Insufficient detail on distribution.</strong> For a new insurer to be viable, it needs a credible distribution strategy. Applications that describe the general approach to distribution without specifying the channels, the key relationships or the expected customer acquisition costs are often queried.</p>
<p><strong>Key individuals without directly relevant experience.</strong> A proposed chief executive who has general financial services experience but limited insurance-specific background will attract questions from MAS about how the insurer will be led competently. This is particularly important for the appointed actuary role, where the regulatory requirements for qualifications and experience are specific.</p>
<p><strong>Capital projections that are too thin.</strong> Capital projections that show the insurer meeting the regulatory minimum with little headroom, or that project rapid growth without adequate capital support, will be questioned. MAS expects new insurers to be well-capitalised relative to the risks they are taking on.</p>
<h2>Preparing the Application</h2>
<p>A well-prepared MAS insurance licence application typically requires four to six months of preparation before submission, depending on the complexity of the proposed business and the starting point.</p>
<p><strong>Business plan development.</strong> The business plan is the foundation of the application. It needs to be specific about the products to be written, the target customers, the distribution strategy, the competitive positioning and the financial projections. Generic business plans that could apply to any insurer are not persuasive.</p>
<p><strong>Key individual appointments.</strong> Key individual appointments should be confirmed and the fit and proper documentation prepared before submission. Late changes to key individuals after submission -- because the proposed CEO has not confirmed their availability or because a director has withdrawn -- create delays and raise questions.</p>
<p><strong>Governance framework development.</strong> Board and management governance frameworks, risk management frameworks, AML/CFT policies, compliance frameworks and operational procedures need to be developed specifically for the proposed business. They should be complete enough to be operational on day one, not frameworks that will be developed after the licence is granted.</p>
<p><strong>Capital structure and financial projections.</strong> The regulatory capital projections need to be prepared to a standard consistent with the RBC2 framework. This includes modelling the capital requirements under the proposed business plan, identifying the capital resources the applicant will provide, and stress testing the capital position.</p>
<p><strong>Regulatory submissions.</strong> The formal submission to MAS must be complete and correctly structured. Incomplete submissions -- missing documents, unsigned declarations, sections left blank -- result in immediate queries that delay the process.</p>
<h2>Working With an Adviser</h2>
<p>Most first-time MAS licence applicants engage external regulatory advisory support. This is particularly valuable for:</p>
<p><strong>Scoping the application correctly</strong> -- understanding which licence category is appropriate, what the regulatory requirements are for that category, and how the application should be structured.</p>
<p><strong>Preparing governance frameworks</strong> -- developing frameworks that are genuinely tailored to the business rather than generic templates.</p>
<p><strong>Preparing the financial projections</strong> -- ensuring that the capital projections are consistent with the RBC2 framework and reflect a realistic view of the business.</p>
<p><strong>Managing the application process</strong> -- coordinating the multiple workstreams involved in an application, tracking outstanding items, and managing the interaction with MAS during the review process.</p>
<p>The value of an experienced adviser is in reducing the number of query rounds with MAS by ensuring the submission is complete and credible from the outset. A well-prepared application that moves through one query round rather than three can save several months on the overall timeline.</p>
