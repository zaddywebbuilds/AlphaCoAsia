Setting Up a Captive Insurer in Singapore: Using the MAS 2026 Compliance Toolkit

A step-by-step guide for regional groups setting up a captive insurer in Singapore, from licence route and application package to governance and capital. Shows how to use the MAS captive compliance toolkit, revised in June 2026, from day one.

To set up a captive insurer in Singapore, a corporate group applies to the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) for a captive insurer licence under the Insurance Act 1966, establishes a locally incorporated or registered entity with a substantive Singapore presence, and builds the governance, risk and compliance framework that MAS expects of a licensed insurer. MAS has indicated that it aims to process captive applications within about three months of a complete submission, and it publishes a captive compliance toolkit, last revised on 22 June 2026, that lists the ongoing obligations a licensed captive carries. This guide walks through the set-up sequence and shows how to use that toolkit from day one rather than after licensing.

Timelines and figures in this article reflect MAS public guidance as we understand it at the date of publication. Confirm every threshold and processing target against the current MAS website before relying on it in a board paper.

Why Regional Groups Choose a Singapore Captive Domicile in Asia

A captive is a licensed insurer that underwrites the risks of its parent and related group companies. Groups use one to retain predictable, frequency-driven risks, smooth premium volatility, gain direct access to the reinsurance market and centralise the management of risk financing across several countries.

Singapore attracts regional groups because it is a regulated, onshore financial centre with a deep reinsurance and broking market, a stable legal system and proximity to the operating entities being insured. A Singapore captive is regulated by MAS under the same Insurance Act that governs direct insurers, which gives reinsurers and fronting insurers comfort about governance and solvency. The trade-off is that MAS expects a real insurer: a licence is not a shell registration.

Interest in the domicile has also been lifted by policy developments. On 7 July 2026 MAS consulted on a legislative framework for protected cell companies, naming captive insurance among the initial use cases alongside insurance-linked securities. The regime is not yet in force, so groups should plan a standard captive today and treat any cell-based structure as a possible later option. Our article on the MAS collateralised reinsurance and ILS consultation covers that proposal in more detail.

Step 1: Confirm a Captive Licence Is the Right Route

Before drafting anything, confirm that the captive category fits the intended business. A captive underwrites group risks. It is not designed to write insurance for unrelated third parties, so a group that expects to sell cover to customers or business partners should revisit its licence category. The comparison of direct insurer, reinsurer, captive and authorised reinsurer routes is set out in our guide to Singapore insurer licence types.

Equally important is whether a captive is the right financing tool at all. A feasibility study should cover the group's premium spend by line, loss history, the lines suited to retention, the reinsurance programme the captive would sit behind, and whether local fronting is required in countries where insurance must be placed with a locally admitted insurer. This study also becomes the factual base for the business plan that MAS will read.

Step 2: Build the Application Package

The licence application is where most delay arises, and delay usually traces to an incomplete or internally inconsistent package. In practice the package needs the following.

  • A business plan describing the group, the risks to be insured, the underwriting and retention strategy, the reinsurance programme and the rationale for using a captive.
  • Financial projections, usually over three to five years, including premium, claims, expenses, reinsurance recoveries and the solvency position under base and stressed assumptions.
  • The proposed capital structure and the source of capital, with evidence that the parent can support the captive if it needs more capital.
  • Shareholder, director and key appointment information, with fit and proper declarations for each person. MAS assesses these against its fit and proper requirements; see our guidance on MAS fit and proper assessment for insurers.
  • A description of the governance structure, including board composition, committees where applicable, the use of a captive manager and the delegation arrangements.
  • Draft risk management, compliance and internal control frameworks proportionate to the captive's scale.

Pre-filing discussions with MAS are valuable. Raising structural questions, such as whether a particular fronting arrangement or a multi-country programme raises concerns, before submission is far cheaper than answering them after the clock has started.

Step 3: Design Governance Around a Small Footprint

Captives are usually small. A typical captive may have a handful of staff or none at all, with underwriting, accounting and administration delegated to a licensed captive manager. MAS accepts delegation, but accountability stays with the board of the captive.

The board needs enough members with relevant insurance, risk or financial experience to challenge the manager, and at least one individual who can credibly oversee the reinsurance and claims arrangements. The management agreement should state which functions the manager performs, the service standards, the reporting lines, the audit and access rights of the captive and of MAS, and exit arrangements. Many first-time sponsors underestimate this point, then discover during licensing that the board cannot explain how it oversees the manager.

Group-level issues deserve explicit treatment: conflicts of interest where directors also hold group roles, arm's length pricing of intra-group premiums, and the interaction with group tax and transfer pricing positions, on which sponsors should take separate tax advice.

Step 4: Plan Capital, Solvency and Reserving

A captive is subject to MAS capital and solvency requirements, calibrated for its risk profile. Sponsors should take the current minimum capital and solvency figures from the MAS captive toolkit and then add a management buffer so the entity does not run close to its limits after a bad year.

Where the captive writes long-tail liability, or relies heavily on a single reinsurer, the capital plan needs to show how the entity stays solvent under stressed loss scenarios. Reserving practice, reinsurance credit risk and concentration limits should be written into the risk framework rather than left as unwritten assumptions. Captives holding material liabilities will usually need actuarial input on reserving and capital, and our actuarial consulting team supports this type of work for licensed entities.

Step 5: Use the MAS Captive Compliance Toolkit From Day One

MAS publishes compliance toolkits for different categories of regulated insurance entity, including captive insurers. The captive toolkit consolidates, in one place, the requirements flowing from the Insurance Act 1966, its subsidiary legislation and the MAS notices and guidelines that apply. Its latest revision date is 22 June 2026.

Most applicants read the toolkit only after licensing. A better approach is to use it during the application to test whether the proposed operating model can meet each obligation. Work through the toolkit item by item and record, for each requirement, the owner, the evidence the captive will hold, the frequency and the escalation route. The output is a compliance register that can double as an exhibit showing MAS that the captive understands its obligations.

Three practical points apply. First, assign each obligation to either the captive board or the captive manager in writing, and align that allocation with the management agreement. Second, set a recurring review of the toolkit so that the register is updated when MAS revises it, as it did in June 2026. Third, include regulatory notifications and approvals in the calendar, such as changes in directors, controllers or key appointments, so that no event is missed because everyone assumed the manager had it. Our walkthrough of the MAS compliance toolkit for insurers and captives shows how to build that register.

Step 6: Prepare for Ongoing Supervision

After licensing, a captive remains a regulated insurer. Expect to maintain statutory returns and regulatory reporting, audited financial statements, board minutes that evidence real challenge, and a documented approach to risk management and solvency assessment proportionate to the captive's size. Where MAS requirements on the own risk and solvency assessment apply to the entity, the board must be able to show ownership of the process rather than simply receiving a document from the manager.

Technology and outsourcing risk also apply. If the captive manager hosts the captive's data and systems, the captive board should understand where the data sits, how continuity is tested and how the arrangement could be exited. Anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism obligations should be confirmed against the toolkit, since the scope of requirements depends on the nature of the business written.

Common Reasons Captive Applications Stall

Several patterns recur. The business plan says little about why a captive is needed, so MAS cannot see the commercial logic. Projections do not reconcile with the reinsurance programme. Proposed directors lack relevant experience and the manager effectively runs the entity. The capital plan has no buffer. Or the applicant has not mapped its obligations, so the operating model has gaps that surface in questions from MAS. Each of these is avoidable with preparation before filing.
